     Good Data Visualization: "PANTONE Colours of the Year"

     Good:

    I classify this as a good visualization because each of the visualizations are visually pleasing, easily perceptible, and substantively based on real data. For the first visualization, the image of a raincloud with the different PANTONE colours appearing as raindrops is very creative, visually striking, and easy to interpret. For the second set of visualizations, which sort the colours by family, decade, and chroma/lightness, the colour swatches on the right side of the graphs provide an accessible reference point for interpreting the graphs, while also directly linking to the data source (Pantone’s various webpages explaining their colours of the year), allowing a viewer to verify the data’s accuracy if they would like to. For the third visualization, I like the addition of summaries of PANTONE’s explanation for each colour of the year. This set of visualizations ultimately provides a comprehensive picture of the available data for PANTONE’s colours of the year.

     Bad:

    I classify this as a bad visualization because it has varying problems related to aesthetics, perceptibility, and substantivity of the data and overarching argument. In terms of aesthetics and perceptibility, the choice to include all genres at once in the genre-publication visualization slides is not visually pleasing, and also makes the meaning of the visualization hard to understand. While adding colour to specific genre lines on the subsequent slides helps, it is still not visually pleasing to have to seek out the coloured lines amidst the many overlapping gray ones. Then, in the top 100 graphic novels visualization, a similar problem emerges as each novel is simply represented by a gray dot, requiring a lot of effort on the viewer’s part to click the different dots to find out the novel titles, an effort that is not sufficiently motivated by the unappealing visuals. 

    In terms of the substantivity of the data and argument, firstly, it is somewhat confusing that “fiction” is repeatedly counted as a graphic novel genre in the same sense as genres like “fantasy” and “science fiction”. Also, “fiction” is noted as being the top genre among the “top 3 genres by book count” in graphic novels, with 79% of the count, while “science fiction” is listed 3rd, with 21% of the count. If fiction comprises 79% of the count, however, would nonfiction not comprise 21% of the count, and thereby be tied with science fiction? Secondly, there is an overarching argument that Marvel and DC film and television adaptations drive public perceptions that graphic novels are predominantly superhero narratives. Many Marvel and DC film and television projects are adapted from comics, however, not graphic novels, and so this distinction between comics and graphic novels should be more clearly defined and explained in relation to the maker's argument.

    - How could this data visualization have been improved?  

     Good:

    Firstly, it is confusing that in the timeline views at the beginning and end, the two years that had a dual colour of the year show these colors above and below each other, pulling the colours out of line from the other year colours. This pulls the viewer’s eye to those two years in a way that is distracting. Splitting the singular color circle down the middle to include both colours would improve the visual consistency and appeal of these visualizations. Secondly, also in the last visualization, when clicking colours to see the cultural explanation for why they were chosen, some colours display only the explanation for that one colour/year, while other colours display explanations for the preceding and following years. This could be cleaned up for consistency and readability.

     Bad:

    Firstly, the genre-publication visualization should be split up so that only the relevant lines are visible on each slide, without all of the overlapping gray lines in the background. Secondly, the top 100 graphic novels visualization should have differently coloured dots, or perhaps even graphic icons that indicate something about the corresponding novel, in order to make prolonged engagement with the visualization more appealing. Thirdly, the author should more clearly define comics versus graphic novels, and be more clear about how this distinction is driving the argument they are making.
